# Research brief: facial symmetry test (what-facial-symmetry-really-shows)

Date: 2026-11-16. Primary keyword: facial symmetry test.

## Intent
Informational with a tool-use edge. The searcher wants to know: is my face symmetrical, is some asymmetry normal, how do I check from a photo correctly, why do results change between photos, does symmetry matter for attractiveness, what do I do with the result, and when is asymmetry a medical question.

## Gaps
- Tool pages show precise-looking scores ("96%", "PERFECT") and say almost nothing about why the same face scores differently across photos.
- Only one competitor (Healthline) gives a home method, and it is a marker-and-ruler routine with no word on camera distance, head turn or expression.
- None puts the research on symmetry and attractiveness next to the clinical view of normal asymmetry, and none explains that the research is mixed.
- None tells the reader what to do with the result, or when asymmetry is a medical question rather than a styling one.

## Information gain plan
1. A table of what distorts a photo test, with the one hard number available (selfie distance), and a fix for each.
2. A table of what the research found, with the limits of each study.
3. A repeatable "take it twice" method that separates camera effects from the face.
4. A comparison of mirror, printed photo and ruler, app, and clinician.
5. Honest limits and red-flag callout placed before any call to action.

## Verified facts (all opened 2026-11-16)
- Penn Medicine, Facial asymmetry (https://www.pennmedicine.org/conditions/facial-asymmetry): "Most people have some degree of facial asymmetry, and this is completely normal." Causes listed: genetics, trauma, neurological and muscular conditions, medical and congenital conditions, dental work, aging, environmental influences and lifestyle habits. Clinicians may use photos and imaging (x-rays, 2D cephalometric radiographs, 3D cone-beam CT).
- Healthline, Asymmetrical Face (https://www.healthline.com/health/asymmetrical-face), medically reviewed by Karen Gill, M.D., last updated 2023-04-20: almost everyone has some asymmetry; home test uses a printed photo, marks on eye corners, lip corners, widest face and nose points, and a ruler for level lines; warns about free apps; sudden droop or numbness needs immediate care; asymmetry tends to increase with age; no clinical research backs facial exercises for symmetry.
- NHS, Bell's palsy (https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/bells-palsy/): call 999 if somebody's face droops on one side, they cannot lift both arms and keep them there, or have difficulty speaking; these can be signs of a stroke.
- Chou et al. 2019, J Clin Med 8(5):648 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6571690/): 3D scans of 600 normal, healthy Taiwanese children aged 6 to 12; mean fluctuating asymmetry 0.96 +/- 0.52 mm; "the normal pediatric face is asymmetric"; perfect bilateral symmetry "almost never exists". Limit: children, one population, 3D stereophotogrammetry.
- Swaddle and Cuthill 1995, Proc R Soc B 261:111-116 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7644543/): when asymmetry was manipulated without changing mean feature size, faces made more symmetrical were rated less attractive; authors suggest removing natural directional asymmetries may make faces look "unemotional".
- Perrett et al. 1999, Evol Hum Behav 20(5):295-307 (https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/symmetry-and-human-facial-attractiveness/): varying only face shape and holding skin texture constant, more symmetrical shape raised attractiveness ratings for male and female faces; earlier opposite results may be artifacts of image processing.
- Komori, Kawamura and Ishihara 2009, Acta Psychologica 131(2):136-142, via BPS Research Digest (https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/beauty-symmetry-versus-averageness): 96 faces measured, 114 raters; for women only averageness predicted attractiveness; for men both averageness and symmetry did.
- Bryan, Perona and Adolphs 2012, PLoS ONE 7(9):e45301 (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0045301): photos taken from within personal space got lower trustworthiness, competence and attractiveness ratings than photos from farther away; effect held controlling for image size, expression and lighting; from close, the nose looks relatively larger and ears smaller.
- Ward et al. 2018, JAMA Facial Plast Surg, via ScienceDaily (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/03/180301115349.htm): at 12 inches the nasal base appeared about 30% wider and the tip about 7% wider than at 5 feet; the article says selfies should not be used to judge nose size.
