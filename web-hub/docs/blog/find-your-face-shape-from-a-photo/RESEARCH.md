# Research brief: find-your-face-shape-from-a-photo

Date: 2026-11-16. Primary keyword: "face shape". Targets: how to find your face shape, face shape from photo, face shape test, face shape for hairstyles and glasses.

## Intent

A reader who searches this wants a quick, believable answer to "what is my face shape", usually with one photo in hand, then wants to know what to do with it (haircut, glasses, earrings). Secondary worry: "is my result right?" because quizzes and apps disagree.

## Competitor outlines and gaps

- MasterClass, PureWow, Man of Many: one H2 per shape, celebrity examples, "3 steps" (widest part, jaw, length). No tables, no numbers, no photo-quality advice, no limits.
- All About Vision, Warby Parker: frame advice per shape, plus fit. They state it is "not a rigid rulebook" and that fit matters. No measurement method.
- Perfect Corp, Oakgen: tool pitches; Oakgen has good photo advice (stand back, eye level, hair off outline) but no sourced numbers.
- Nobody notes that the sources disagree on the definition of the oval (see facts). Nobody offers a table that turns measurements into a shape, nobody cites the clinical facial index, and nobody cites the selfie-distance study.

## Information-gain plan

1. A measurement checklist table (what to mark on the photo, where, what it tells you).
2. A clinical facial-index table (Bannister cutoffs) so "longer than wide" has a number, with its honest limit.

4. A section on why photos mislead (selfie distance, with the actual study numbers and its limits).
5. Plain statement that shape definitions disagree between sources, and what to do about it.

## Verified facts (all read 2026-11-16)

- Selfie distance: Ward B, Ward M, Fried O, Paskhover B. "Nasal Distortion in Short-Distance Photographs: The Selfie Effect." JAMA Facial Plast Surg, online March 1, 2018, doi 10.1001/jamafacial.2018.0009 (full text PMC5876805). Mathematical model using average measurements from a diverse US sample, not patients. At 12 inches (30 cm) perceived nasal breadth relative to bizygomatic breadth increases 30% in males and 29% in females versus an orthographic projection; at 5 feet (1.5 m) essentially no difference. Authors: the distortion "does not accurately reflect the 3-dimensional appearance of the nose." It modeled the nose only, nothing about jaws.
- Facial index: Choudhary U et al., "An Anthropometric Study of the Facial Index Among Undergraduate Medical Students at the Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, Uttar Pradesh," Cureus 18(6):e111443, 2026-06-24. FI = morphological facial height (nasion to gnathion) / bizygomatic breadth x 100. Bannister classification: hypereuryprosopic below 79.9, euryprosopic (broad) 80.0 to 84.9, mesoprosopic 85.0 to 89.9, leptoprosopic (long) 90.0 to 95.0, hyperleptoprosopic above 95.0. In 200 students: mesoprosopic 34.5%, euryprosopic 28.5%, leptoprosopic 15.0%, hypereuryprosopic 11.0%, hyperleptoprosopic 11.0%. Males mean 88.15, females 85.74. One Indian student sample.
- Shape count: MasterClass (last updated 2022-12-21) names six common shapes: diamond, heart, oval, rectangle, round, square; Warby Parker (updated 2026-08-13) names five (no rectangle).
- Oval definition disagrees: MasterClass says the forehead is usually the widest part and the face is longer than wide; Warby Parker says longer than wide with a softly rounded jaw and forehead; Hair.com says "An oval face is wider than it is long"; Glam says round and oval are both widest at the cheeks.
- Warby Parker: "It's not a rigid rulebook." Round: angular frames, rectangular or square, cat-eye. Square: round or oval, lighter frames. Oval: frames as wide as or wider than cheekbones. Heart: frames slightly wider than the forehead, rounded or oval, semi-rimless that keeps the bottom light. Diamond: cat-eye, browline, avoid narrow or low-sitting frames. "Fit and Feel Matter, Too."
- All About Vision (author Martha Elaine Belden, updated 2026-05-19): you still need facial measurements and pupillary distance to order the right size; shape terms "relatively standard"; heart with high cheekbones suits circular frames; round suits full-rimmed rectangular frames.
- Hair.com (Bertram K, L'Oreal Professionnel portfolio artist): aim for the illusion of an oval; volume is the main tool ("Graduation adds volume, whereas layers diminish it"); consider hair texture and body proportions. Examples: square, lob with bangs; long, layers at cheekbones and jaw; heart, rounded pageboy; round, curly shag; oval, textured pixie; diamond, wavy chin-length bob.
- Glam (Vanessa Elle, 2025-04-30, stylist Joseph Katz): face shape is a consideration but "the overall outfit and cohesive look should take precedence." Drop earrings for round and oval, hoops for square and rectangular, teardrops for heart and diamond.
- Necklines: no credible tested source found; not stated as a rule.
